# Usability Principles

Sources, credited in full:
- Steve Krug, *Don't Make Me Think, Revisited: A Common Sense Approach to Web
  Usability* (3rd ed., New Riders, 2014).
- Don Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things, Revised and Expanded Edition*
  (Basic Books, 2013).

Summaries are in my own words; short quotes are marked. Each rule is: **the
rule**, then why, then one web example. To review a page, glance at it like a
hurried stranger, walk Part 1 (can people tell what this is and what to click),
then Part 2 (can they act, see what happened, and recover), then the checklist.

## Part 1: Krug, Don't Make Me Think

### 1. Make each page self-evident

**Rule: A page should be obvious at a glance. If it cannot be obvious, it must
at least explain itself with very little reading.**
Why: Krug's First Law of Usability is "Don't make me think." Every small
question in the visitor's head ("Is that a button? What does 'Solutions'
mean?") costs attention and goodwill, and those costs add up.

Example: A button labeled "Jobs" beats one labeled "Career-Opportunities Hub."
Nobody has to stop and translate "Jobs."

### 2. Design for scanning, not reading

**Rule: Assume people glance at the page, pick out a few words, and click the
first thing that looks close enough.**
Why: Krug's facts of life. People scan instead of reading. They
satisfice: they pick the first reasonable option rather than the best one,
because guessing wrong costs little (the Back button). They muddle through
without learning how things really work.

Example: Put the key word first in each link and heading. "Pricing for teams"
scans better than "Learn more about how our plans work for teams."

### 3. Design billboards, not brochures

**Rule: Treat each page like a billboard seen at highway speed.**
Krug's billboard list: use conventions, build a clear hierarchy, split the
page into clear areas, make clickable things obvious, cut noise, and format
text for scanning (headings, short paragraphs, bullets, key terms bold).

Why: Visitors decide in seconds whether a page is worth their time. A page
that cannot be read in that window gets skipped.

Example: A hero with one headline, one sentence, and one button reads at a
glance. A hero with a carousel, three buttons, and a paragraph does not.

### 4. Use conventions

**Rule: Put things where people expect them and make them look the way people
expect. Innovate only when your version is clearly better.**
Why: Conventions are free knowledge. A logo top left that links home, a
search box with a magnifier, a cart top right: none of these need explaining.
Clarity beats cleverness. If you break a convention, the gain must be large
enough to pay for the confusion. Norman agrees: when no natural mapping
exists, standardize.

Example: "Sign in" belongs in the top right corner. Moving it into a footer to
"keep the header clean" means returning users hunt for it.

### 5. Build a clear visual hierarchy

**Rule: The more important something is, the more prominent it looks. Things
that are related look related. Things that are nested look nested.**
Why: Hierarchy does the reading for the visitor. It tells them what matters
and how parts relate before they read a word.

Example: The page title is the largest text. A form's fields sit inside one
visual group with its submit button, not floating loose among promos.

### 6. Make clickable things look clickable

**Rule: Nobody should spend even a moment wondering whether something can be
clicked.**
Why: Krug says users should never have to think about whether something is
clickable. Doubt here causes both missed clicks and wasted clicks.

Example: Never style links and headings the same color. Give buttons a shape,
a fill, or a border so they read as buttons, not as labels.

### 7. Keep the noise down

**Rule: Treat every element as noise until it proves it helps.**
Why: Krug names three kinds of noise: shouting (everything bold and bright),
disorganization (no grid, no grouping), and clutter (too much stuff). Noise
hides the one thing the visitor came for.

Example: Remove the badge row, the cookie banner overlap, and the second
promo strip, and the main button suddenly becomes findable.

### 8. Count mindless clicks, not clicks

**Rule: Many easy, unambiguous clicks are fine. One click that requires
thought is expensive.**
Why: Krug's Second Law: "It doesn't matter how many times I have to click, as
long as each click is a mindless, unambiguous choice." Click count is a poor
measure of difficulty.

Example: "Men / Women / Kids" then "Shoes" then "Running" is easy. One menu
with forty mixed categories is hard, even though it is fewer clicks.

### 9. Omit needless words

**Rule: Cut half the words on each page, then cut half of what is left.**
Why: Krug's Third Law. Fewer words reduce noise, make the useful content
stand out, and make pages shorter so more fits on screen. Kill "happy talk"
(welcome text that says nothing) and instructions nobody reads.

Example: Replace "Welcome to our platform! We are thrilled to help you
discover..." with one line that says what the site does.

### 10. Navigation: street signs and breadcrumbs

**Rule: Persistent navigation should answer, on every page, where am I, what
is here, and how do I get elsewhere.**
Why: The web has no sense of scale, direction, or location. Navigation
replaces those missing senses. It also shows what the site contains and how
it is organized, which builds confidence.

Example: Every page shows the site ID (linking home), the main sections, a
"you are here" marker on the current section, and search if the site is big.
Page names match the link that was clicked to get there.

### 11. Run the trunk test

**Rule: Drop yourself on a random page, squint, and try to point out the site
ID, page name, main sections, local options, "you are here" marker, and
search.**
Why: Visitors often arrive deep inside a site from search, social links, or
email. They never saw your home page. If those six items do not pop out
without close study, people feel lost.

Example: A blog post reached from a newsletter should still show the brand,
the post title, and a clear way to the rest of the site.

### 12. Home page: what is this, what can I do here, why here

**Rule: The home page must answer three questions within seconds: what is
this site, what can I do here, and why should I use it instead of somewhere
else.**
Why: The "main point" is the one thing insiders never notice is missing,
because they already know it. Use a tagline beside the logo, a short welcome
line, and show where to start. Use as much space as needed, and no more.

Example: A tagline like "Searchable archive of real marketing emails" next to
the logo beats a motto like "Inspiration, delivered." A motto is not a
description.

Also: make entry points (search, browse, sign in) look like entry points and
label them plainly. A good tagline is six to eight words that only you could
say. Never use a mission statement as the welcome blurb. Protect the page from
promo creep; over-promotion kills what made it work.

### 13. Guard the reservoir of goodwill

**Rule: Every visitor arrives with a limited store of goodwill. Every hassle
drains it. Every courtesy refills it.**
Why: When the reservoir empties, people leave and think less of you.

Drains it: hiding what people want (prices, contact, what sign-in gets them),
rejecting input formatted the "wrong" way, asking for data you do not need,
fake sincerity, marketing filler in the way, a sloppy or abandoned look.

Refills it: making the main tasks obvious and easy, telling people what they
want to know up front, saving steps, answering likely questions honestly,
easy error recovery, apologizing when you cannot help.

Example: A form that asks only for an email address keeps goodwill. One that
also demands company size, phone, and role for a free download drains it.

### 14. Mobile: tradeoffs, not shrinking

**Rule: On small screens, keep the frequent and urgent things close at hand
and everything else a few obvious taps away. Never trade away usability to
save space.**
Why: Mobile design is mostly tradeoffs. Touch has no hover, so anything that
relied on hover (tooltips, hover menus, hover-only reveal) is invisible. Flat
styling can strip away the cues that show what is tappable. Speed matters
more because connections vary.

Example: Allow pinch zoom. Keep tap targets large. Do not hide the main button
behind a menu icon. Provide a path to the full site. When a link from email
lands on mobile, take people to that item, not to the home page.

### 15. Accessibility is mostly usability first

**Rule: First fix what confuses everyone. Then take the easy wins.**
Why: Anything that confuses sighted mouse users confuses screen reader users
more, and they have a harder time recovering. Screen reader users also scan:
they listen to the first few words of a link or line and move on.

Krug's easy wins: useful alt text (empty alt on decorative images), real
heading order, a `<label>` on every field, a "Skip to main content" link,
full keyboard use, strong text contrast.

Example: A button that reads "Open the archive" is announced clearly. An icon
button with no label is announced as "button."

### 16. Test it yourself, cheaply and often

**Rule: Watch three real people try to use the thing, once a month, and fix the
worst problems first.**
Why: There is no Average User, and debates about what people like go nowhere.
Watching people resolves arguments about what works. Three users find most of
the serious problems. Testing early and small beats one big test late.

How: give each person a task, not a tour. Ask them to think aloud and do not
help. Debrief the same day and pick the few worst problems. Fix by removing
or tweaking, not by adding. Focus groups are not usability tests: opinions are
not behavior.

Example: Show the landing page to someone outside the team for five seconds,
then ask "What is this site and what would you do first?"

## Part 2: Norman, The Design of Everyday Things

Norman's core idea: when people struggle with a design, the design is at fault,
not the person. Good design makes the possible actions discoverable and the
results understandable.

### 17. Affordances: what actions are possible

**Rule: Make sure the thing can actually do what people will try to do with
it.**
Why: An affordance is a relationship between a thing and a person: what the
person can do with it. A real affordance that nobody perceives is useless.

Example: A whole product card should be clickable if people will click
anywhere on it. If only the tiny title link works, the card fails them.

### 18. Signifiers: show where and how to act

**Rule: Add visible signals that say what action is possible and where to do
it.**
Why: Norman separates affordances (what is possible) from signifiers (the cues
that tell you). On screens, almost everything is a signifier: underlines,
button shapes, arrows, labels, placeholder text, cursors. Signifiers must be
perceivable or they do not exist.

Example: A chevron on an accordion row, a text cursor in a search field, the
word "Search" on the button. A "Norman door" on the web is a flat text label
that turns out to be the only way forward.

### 19. Mapping: controls sit near, and match, what they affect

**Rule: Place each control next to the thing it changes, and lay controls out
the way their effects are laid out.**
Why: Good mapping lets people predict results without memorizing anything.
Norman ranks it: controls on the object are best, controls right beside it
next best, controls arranged in the same layout after that.

Example: Put a "Remove" link on each list item rather than one "Remove
selected" button at the top. Put sort controls directly above the column
they sort.

### 20. Feedback: show what happened, right away

**Rule: Every action gets an immediate, informative, proportionate response.**
Why: Without feedback, people repeat the action, give up, or assume it failed.
Norman says even a tenth of a second of delay can feel off. Feedback must say
what happened. Too much feedback is as bad as too little: constant alerts get
ignored, including the important ones.

Example: After a form submit, the button shows a spinner and disables, then
the form is replaced with "Thanks, we got your request for Glossier." A
generic toast that vanishes in a second is weak feedback.

### 21. Conceptual models: help people build the right picture

**Rule: Make the way the system works visible, so people form a simple,
useful mental picture that matches reality.**
Why: People act on their own model of how something works. A wrong model
causes wrong actions that feel right. The model is built from what the design
shows: layout, labels, behavior, and what happens after each step.

Example: If content is gated, say so before the click: "Free account needed
to browse." A visitor who expects an open gallery and hits a sign-in wall
builds the wrong model and feels tricked.

### 22. Constraints: make the wrong action hard

**Rule: Limit choices so the right action is the natural one.**
Why: Norman names four kinds. Physical (it will not fit), cultural
(conventions), semantic (meaning of the situation), logical (only one option
remains). Constraints reduce what people must remember and prevent errors
before they happen. A forcing function blocks the next step until something
is done.

Example: Disable "Submit" until required fields are filled, and say which
ones. Use a date picker so the date cannot be malformed. Use an email input
type so the phone keyboard shows "@".

### 23. Put knowledge in the world, not in the head

**Rule: Show people what they need at the moment they need it. Do not make
them remember it.**
Why: Memory is short and unreliable. Visible options, labels, and reminders
let beginners succeed and help experts on rare tasks.

Example: Show password rules next to the field before typing, not after a
failed attempt. Keep a visible label on each field instead of placeholder
text that disappears.

### 24. The seven stages of action

**Rule: Check that the design answers each of the seven questions a person
asks while acting.**
Norman's cycle is one goal, three steps of doing, three of checking: (1) goal,
what do I want? (2) plan, what are the options? (3) specify, what can I do
now? (4) perform, how do I do it? (5) perceive, what happened? (6) interpret,
what does it mean? (7) compare, did I reach my goal?

Why: Each stage is a place where a person can get stuck. Information that
helps them act is feedforward. Information that helps them understand the
result is feedback.

Example: A request form passes stage 3 if the form is visible, stage 4 if
labels are clear, stage 5 if a confirmation appears, and stage 7 if the
confirmation says what happens next ("We will email you when it is added").

### 25. Bridge the gulfs of execution and evaluation

**Rule: Shrink the gap between "I want to" and "I know how" (execution), and
between "I did something" and "I know what happened" (evaluation).**
Why: The gulf of execution is bridged with signifiers, constraints, mapping,
and a conceptual model. The gulf of evaluation is bridged with feedback and a
conceptual model. Wide gulfs make people blame themselves, which is wrong:
the design is at fault.

Example: Execution gap: a visitor wants to see sample content but the only
button says "Get started." Evaluation gap: they sign in and land on a blank
dashboard with no sign it worked.

### 26. Design for error

**Rule: Assume people will make every possible error. Prevent what you can,
make the rest easy to spot and easy to fix.**
Why: Norman separates slips (right goal, wrong action, like a mis-tap) from
mistakes (wrong goal or plan, often from a wrong model). Slips are prevented
with spacing, constraints, and distinct controls. Mistakes are prevented with
clear models and feedback. Norman's advice:

- Prefer undo over "Are you sure?" People confirm on autopilot. If you must
  confirm, name both the action and the object it affects.
- Run sensibility checks: question values that make no sense.
- Treat an error as a near miss. Keep what was entered, guide the fix, never
  make people start over. Help, do not scold.
- Keep destructive controls away from common ones.

Example: A form with one bad field keeps every other value, highlights the
field, and says "That email is missing an @." A form that clears itself and
says "Invalid input" fails every point above.
## Landing Page Review Checklist

Use this on any landing page, sign-up page, or home page. Each line names the
rule numbers above that back it up.

**First five seconds (1, 2, 3, 12)**

- [ ] After five seconds a stranger can say what the site is, what they can
      do here, and what to do first.
- [ ] The headline describes the product. It is not a motto or slogan.
- [ ] There is a reason to use this instead of the alternatives.
- [ ] A tagline beside the logo says what the site is in under ten words.

**The main call to action (5, 6, 18, 21, 25)**

- [ ] One primary button. It is the most prominent thing in the hero.
- [ ] The button looks like a button: shape, fill, contrast.
- [ ] The label names the outcome ("Open the archive"), not a vague verb
      ("Get started", "Submit").
- [ ] It is clear what happens on click: sign in, a form, a new page.
- [ ] If sign-in is required, the page says so before the click, and says what
      sign-in costs (free? email only?).
- [ ] Secondary actions look clearly secondary.

**Hierarchy, noise, words (5, 7, 9)**

- [ ] Most important item is biggest. Related items are grouped.
- [ ] No happy talk. Words cut by half, then cut again.
- [ ] Each section has a scannable heading with its key word first.
- [ ] Nothing competes with the main button: no carousels, no promo pileups.

**Trust and goodwill (13, 21)**

- [ ] Real samples of what is behind the gate, so visitors know what they get.
- [ ] Who it is for and what it costs, said plainly. No surprise paywall.
- [ ] Signs of life: recent items, counts, last updated.

**Navigation and orientation (4, 10, 11)**

- [ ] Logo top left linking home; "Sign in" top right for returning users.
- [ ] A visitor landing on any page passes the trunk test.

**Forms (13, 20, 22, 23, 26)**

- [ ] Ask only for what is needed. Visible labels, not placeholder-only.
- [ ] Accept any reasonable format; use input types and pickers to block bad
      input.
- [ ] Errors sit next to the field, in plain words, and keep the rest of the
      input.
- [ ] Submit shows instant feedback, blocks double submits, then confirms
      what happens next.

**Mobile (14)**

- [ ] Main button visible, not behind a menu. Large tap targets, no hover-only
      cues. Zoom works. Fast on a slow connection.

**Accessibility (15)**

- [ ] One `h1`, headings in order. Alt text where it informs, empty alt where
      it decorates.
- [ ] Every field has a `<label>`; buttons have text. Keyboard works with
      visible focus. Strong contrast.

**Before shipping (16, 24)**

- [ ] Walk the seven questions for the main task: goal, options, what now, how,
      what happened, what it means, did it work.
- [ ] Watch three outside people try the main task. Fix the worst problem
      first.
