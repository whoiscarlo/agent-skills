# Conversion Copy

A playbook for copy that has one job: get the next action taken. Distilled from
Schwartz's *Breakthrough Advertising*, *Ogilvy on Advertising*, three
conversion/UX studies, and current (2025-26) web research.

Sibling skills: `offer-crafting` (what you promise), `lead-gen-funnels` (where
this copy sits), `sales-psychology` (why it works), `ux-persuasion` (design side
of the same page), `brand-voice` (voice, one big idea, the buyer's own words),
`clear-writing` (plain sentences), `closing-deals` (pricing and negotiation).

## The two questions to answer before writing anything

1. **What desire already exists?** Copy cannot create desire — it can only
   channel an existing one onto your product (Schwartz ch.1). Pick ONE desire,
   scored on three dimensions: urgency (how badly), staying power (how often it
   recurs), and scope (how many people share it). One headline = one desire.
2. **Sell what the product DOES, not what it is.** Nobody buys the steel in the
   car. Physical facts (specs, stack, team size) only serve later as proof for
   the performance promised up top.

Then diagnose the reader on two axes — awareness and sophistication — and let
those pick the headline. This is the spine of the whole skill.

### Belief comes before copy

The biggest problem in marketing is that nobody believes you. People already
want the result. If they do not buy, they have not been given a reason to
believe. Solve this before headlines, tactics or offers. (Why Don't People
Buy? Because Nobody Believes You)

- **State the facts and tell the truth.** Show what only you can show. Big
  proof lets you say less: standing next to 20,000 five-star reviews, "the
  book's good, look" is enough.
- **If the facts are weak, do not rewrite the copy.** Go create better facts
  first: more results, more reviews, real case numbers. Then state them
  plainly. No polish fixes a claim nobody believes.
- **Never invent a result to fill a headline.** If you do not have the user's
  real number, leave a marked placeholder and say what proof to collect.
- **Record your best live pitch first.** Copy is that pitch written down. If
  the page says something different from what closes people face to face,
  the page is wrong. (Is My Copy Just My Best Sales Pitch, Written Down?)

### Find proven demand before writing

Do not guess topics. Write on what people already search for and upvote.
(How Do I Know What My Customers Want to Read?)

- Copy Google's "searches related to" box into one doc. Group by one word
  ("crate") and answer every question in one large piece.
- Save the most upvoted Reddit and Quora headlines in a swipe file (a saved
  set of proven headlines). 47 upvotes beats 2.
- Borrow the shape, change the subject: "If you can train a chicken..."
  becomes "If you can train a shih tzu...".

## Awareness stage → headline picker (Schwartz ch.2)

"A headline which works for a market in one stage of awareness will not work
for a market in another stage." Match the opening to what the reader already
knows, at the exact point they know it.

| Stage | Reader's head | Headline leads with | Pattern + classic example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Most aware | Knows your product, wants it, hasn't bought | Product name + deal | "Revere Zomar Lens — Formerly $149.50 — Now Only $119.95" |
| 2. Product-aware | Knows the product, not yet sold on it | Product + its superiority (new proof, new mechanism, sharper image, removed limitation) | "At 60 miles an hour the loudest noise in this Rolls-Royce is the electric clock" |
| 3. Solution-aware | Wants the outcome, doesn't know your product delivers it | The desire, crystallized; product enters later | "How to Win Friends and Influence People"; "Who else wants a whiter wash with no hard work?" |
| 4. Problem-aware | Feels the problem, doesn't know solutions exist | The problem, named and dramatized, then the solution | "Corns?"; "Do YOU make these mistakes in English?"; "Shrinks hemorrhoids without surgery" |
| 5. Unaware | Won't admit or can't name the problem | Identification — echo the reader's emotion/identity; no price, no product name, no direct claim | "Why Men Crack…"; "The University of the Night"; "They Laughed When I Sat Down at the Piano—" |

Rules that fall out of the table:

- The less aware the market, the longer the bridge: headline → paragraph 1 →
  paragraph 2, each with the single job of getting the next line read. The
  headline does not have to sell, mention the product, or state the main appeal.
- Stage 5 headlines must still *select* the right reader ("it must be his
  headline, his problem") — reject as many people as they attract. A clever
  attention-getter that anyone could claim is a wasted headline.
- Stage 1 is the only stage where price alone carries the page.
- Markets move up the scale. A winning headline expires when they do.

## Sophistication: how many rivals got there first (Schwartz ch.3)

Awareness asks "what does the reader know about the desire?"; sophistication
asks "how many similar promises have they already heard?"

| Stage | Market condition | Your move | Example (reducing market) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. First in | No one has made the claim | State the claim simply and directly — nothing fancy | "Now! Lose Ugly Fat!" |
| 2. Claim war | Direct claim still works, competitors copying | Enlarge the claim — push it to the believable limit | "Lose up to 47 pounds in 4 weeks — or receive $40 back" |
| 3. Claims exhausted | Every extreme heard; skepticism sets in | Lead with a NEW MECHANISM — how it works, not what it does; claim moves to the subhead | "Floats fat right out of your body!" |
| 4. Mechanism war | Rivals copy the mechanism | Elaborate the mechanism: easier, faster, surer, fewer limits | "First NO-DIET reducing wonder drug!" |
| 5. Burned out | Nothing is believed | Drop claims and mechanisms; lead with identification with the reader (same play as awareness stage 5) | "Why Men Crack…" (revived Postum) |

Diagnosis takes an hour: collect every competing ad/landing page in the space
and see which stage their headlines are written for — then write one stage ahead.

## Headline formula bank

First Ogilvy's ground rules (*Ogilvy on Advertising*, ch.7), then Schwartz's
strengtheners.

**Ogilvy's rules:**
- 5x as many people read the headline as the body. No promise there = 90% of
  the money wasted.
- Promise a benefit. Headlines that promise a benefit are read by four times
  as many people.
- News works: ads with news are recalled by 22% more people. New product, new
  use, improvement — say "amazing, introducing, now, suddenly" only when it IS news.
- Specifics beat generalities: "Sears makes a profit of 5 per cent" out-pulled
  vague trust claims. Numbers are remembered; adjectives are not.
- Flag your prospect by name when the product is for a narrow group: "asthma,"
  "bladder weakness," "women over 35."
- Include the brand name (80% of readers never reach the body copy) — unless
  you're deliberately writing for a stage-5 unaware market (Schwartz overrides
  Ogilvy there, and Ogilvy concedes the point with blind-headline exceptions).
- 10-word headlines sell more merchandise than short ones (retail study);
  long is fine if every word earns its place.
- Never run "blind" headlines that don't say what the product is, and skip
  puns and double meanings — you're not paying readers to decode.

**Schwartz's verbalization moves** (the most useful of the 38, with the
original examples — apply to a claim you already chose):

| Move | Example |
|---|---|
| Measure the claim | "Who ever heard of 17,000 blooms from a single plant?" |
| Measure its speed | "In two seconds, Bayer Aspirin begins to dissolve in your glass" |
| Show the mechanism | "Floats fat right out of your body!" |
| Remove a limitation | "Shrinks hemorrhoids without surgery" |
| Make it a question | "Who else wants a whiter wash — with no hard work?" |
| Offer information | "What everybody ought to know about this stock and bond business" |
| Case history / quote | "Look, Mom — no cavities!" |
| Paradox | "How a bald-headed barber helped save my hair" |
| Demonstrate with a prime example | "Jake LaMotta, 160-lb fighter, fails to flatten Mono paper cup" |
| Before-and-after | "Before Coldene… / With Coldene a child gets over a cold in five days" |
| Address the prospect directly | "To the man who will settle for nothing less than the presidency of his firm" |
| Warn about the pitfall | "Don't invest one cent until you check this guide" |
| Easy-threshold challenge | "If you can count to eleven, you can increase your speed at numbers" |
| Name the problem | "When you're weary with day-time fatigue…" |
| Stress exclusivity | "Only Gleem has GL-70…" |
| Ultimate-triumph story | "They laughed when I sat down at the piano — but when I started to play!—" |

Modern rendering: same moves, tighter words, 5th-8th-grade reading level,
outcome first ("Track bugs in half the time" not "v3.0 released").

## Landing page, section by section

One page, one purpose, one call to action (10 Keys). Every element below
either advances the single action or gets cut.

1. **Message match.** Page headline repeats (near-verbatim) the ad / email /
   post that brought the visitor. Mismatch = disorientation = bounce, and a
   worse ad quality score.
2. **Hero headline.** Written for the traffic's awareness stage (table above).
   Benefit or crystallized problem, concrete, no cleverness tax.
3. **Subhead.** One sentence: who it's for + what they get + the mechanism
   or differentiator. Carries what the headline had to leave out.
4. **Primary CTA above the fold.** Visible without scrolling, one of the top
   three things you see when you squint at the page. (Classic advice said put
   *everything* above the fold; current practice: the first screen must carry
   headline + CTA, and the page may run long below — repeat the CTA every
   couple of screens.)
5. **Proof block, early.** Testimonials, logos, subscriber counts, security
   seals. A Verisign seal alone lifted one store's conversions 41%. Experts
   and peers beat celebrities (Ogilvy: the ex-burglar sold the Chubb safe);
   readers remember the celebrity and forget the product.
6. **Body copy.** Ogilvy's rules: write to one person, in everyday spoken
   language; short sentences, short paragraphs; story form beats essay;
   specifics, not superlatives ("brag and boast" convinces nobody); avoid
   analogies (misread) and esoteric words. Long copy is fine for a
   considered purchase — it sells more than short when the reader is a real
   prospect — but every line must pull the next.
7. **Intensify the one desire** (Schwartz ch.7, condensed): put the claim in
   action, bring the reader into the scene, show experts approving, compare
   and prove superiority, show the dark side you rescue them from, show how
   easy it is, then summarize and put the guarantee to work.
8. **Handle price/objections.** State the price or what replaces it ("Free",
   "No card required"). Hiding price reduces selling power (Ogilvy). Redefine
   the objection: simpler than they fear, cheaper than the alternative.
9. **Final CTA + risk reversal.** Guarantee, cancel-anytime, privacy promise.
10. **Strip the exits.** No nav bar, no outbound links, no unrelated offers.
    Links away from the page are leaks (10 Keys #10).
11. **Craft check.** Zero spelling/grammar errors (trust), squint test (does
    the eye land on headline → CTA?), mobile first.

**Every element earns its place** (Will This Line Get More People to Buy?).
Test each line: will it get a higher share of this audience to buy, or bring
in a group who would not have bought? If 100 people see the page and one
phrase speaks to 3 more of them, it pays for itself. Otherwise cut it.

- **Show, do not just tell.** Treat the page like an unboxing video. Show
  each thing they get on screen while the copy names it.
- **One tile per buyer type, each with its own proof.** Shoppers click every
  image like on Amazon. Instead of one generic feature list, give each type
  of buyer a tile with feedback on that exact topic.
- **No testimonials yet? Make them.** Run a session on each topic, ask for
  feedback, and screenshot it.
- **Use the PS.** The headline, first line and PS are the most read parts.
  Use the PS to say who it is not for, so the right buyers lean in.

## CTA copy rules

- **Verb + what they get**, 2-5 words: "Download the free guide", "Create my
  media kit". Never "Submit" or "Click here".
- **Say the cost-free part on the button.** Firefox: "Try Firefox" →
  "Download Now — Free" lifted downloads measurably.
- **First person tests well**: "Start my trial" / "Send me the checklist"
  connects better than second person ("Start your trial"). Test it.
- **One page, one CTA.** Secondary actions (docs, pricing) visually
  subordinate or absent.
- **Click-trigger microcopy** under the button kills the last fear: "No
  credit card required" · "Takes 2 minutes" · "Unsubscribe anytime" · "There
  will be no additional costs" (that last line lifted Yoast checkout
  conversions 11.3%).
- Set expectations in the label when the next step isn't obvious:
  "Download guide — 12-page PDF" beats "Buy now".
- Contrast and placement matter more than any magic color; test color and
  copy together on real traffic.

## Form and friction copy

**Email capture** (lead-gen study + current data):
- Ask for the minimum. One field (email) ≈ 2.1% sign-up vs 1.4% at five+
  fields; ≤3 fields converts ~25% better than longer forms.
- Generic "join our newsletter" boxes convert 0.3-1.2%. What converts 5-20%:
  - **Content upgrade** — a bonus tied to the exact page the reader is on
    (checklist, template, source file). Took LeadPages from 0.5% → 10%.
  - **Social squeeze page** — give the valuable content un-gated, put the
    opt-in directly below it ("want more like this?"). Backlinko: 21.7%.
  - **Feature box** — value proposition + email box front-and-center on the
    home page (DIYthemes: +51.7% daily subscribers).
- The form headline states what they GET and when: "Get the 12-point launch
  checklist (instant PDF)". Add one line of proof ("Join 9,400 creators") and
  one line of safety ("No spam, unsubscribe anytime").
- Incentives work: capture popups offering a discount convert 7.45% vs 4.6%
  without — but test timing/placement; a mistimed popup is the #1 reported
  distraction in abandonment studies.

**Checkout / signup flow copy** (checkout + cart-abandonment studies —
average cart abandonment ≈ 67%):
- Offer **guest checkout**; forced registration is a walk-away moment.
- **No surprise costs.** Show shipping/taxes/total price early and keep a
  running total visible. Surprise fees are the top abandonment trigger.
- Ask for the sensitive stuff (card) **last** — sunk effort carries people
  through the final step.
- Put the promo-code field where people expect it, early — hunting for it
  breaks trust in the price.
- Distinguish sign-in from sign-up loudly (37% of new users start typing
  into the wrong form); on failure, say WHICH field was wrong.
- Trust symbols, reviews, delivery dates, and a reachable human (live chat)
  each measurably reduce abandonment; exit surveys tell you which one you're
  missing (+20% conversions for teams that acted on them).
- Auto-format numbers; never make the user fix your input mask.

## Email subject-line patterns

Same engine as headlines: one desire, matched to awareness, specific.

- **Curiosity + self-interest** (never curiosity alone): "The 5-minute media
  kit trick nobody uses"
- **Named problem**: "Your pitch email is missing one thing"
- **Specific number/result**: "How Ana got 3 brand deals in 30 days"
- **Question that qualifies**: "Still sending rate cards as PDFs?"
- **News**: "New: auto-updating follower counts"
- **Direct offer (most-aware list)**: "Your media kit is ready — 1 click to
  publish"
- Keep it under ~7 words where possible; front-load the operative word; the
  preview line is your subhead — don't waste it on "View in browser".
- Nurture sequence = awareness ladder in miniature: educate (problem-aware) →
  demonstrate mechanism (solution-aware) → offer + urgency (most-aware).

## Paid social ad copy

(Why Do People Scroll Past My Ad?; My Clinic Needs More Patients. How
Should I Advertise?)

- **Make the ad look like a post.** Emojis once looked native; now they
  mark an ad.
- **Call out a label people use for themselves.** If they would not call
  themselves "manifestors", go up or down a level.
- **Open with pain, not a promise.** An if-then line or an implied yes:
  "Do you ever keep trying things and nothing seems to stick?"
- **Benefit of the benefit, then reword the cliche.** "Meal plan" becomes
  "never wonder what's for dinner". "Eliminate guesswork" becomes "the
  easiest thing to eat is also the right thing to eat."
- **Analogies from the reader's world**, and teach in the ad so they
  believe you will help after they pay.
- **Drop expert language.** Sell the outcome (feel better, insurance
  covers it), not the procedure. Hormozi runs copy through the Hemingway
  app to get below fifth grade, ideally below third.

## Current tactics (web, 2026)

Sourced findings from the four searches; contradictions with the classics flagged.

1. Plain language wins measurably: copy at 5th-8th-grade reading level
   converts ~11.1% vs 5.3% for complex copy; one study saw a 24% drop from
   advanced vocabulary ([KlientBoost](https://www.klientboost.com/landing-pages/saas-landing-page/)).
   Agrees with Ogilvy's "language people use in everyday conversation."
2. Number-bearing headlines get higher CTRs; "12x faster revenue recovery"
   beats "save some time" ([fibr.ai](https://fibr.ai/landing-page/saas-landing-pages),
   [GetResponse](https://www.getresponse.com/blog/copywriting-landing-page-conversions)).
   Direct descendant of Schwartz's "measure the claim" and Ogilvy's specifics.
3. ~8 of 10 visitors read the headline, ~2 of 10 read on
   ([VWO](https://vwo.com/blog/landing-page-copywriting/)) — Ogilvy's 5:1
   ratio, still holding.
4. Standard frameworks in current use — AIDA, PAS (problem-agitate-solve),
   voice-of-customer mining ([Landingi](https://landingi.com/conversion-optimization/copywriting/),
   [Copy.ai](https://www.copy.ai/blog/landing-page-copywriting)). PAS is
   Schwartz's problem-aware playbook with a modern name.
5. Well-optimized SaaS pages convert 5-8%; top performers 10%+ via single
   CTA, early social proof, outcome-first headline
   ([KlientBoost](https://www.klientboost.com/landing-pages/saas-landing-page/)).
6. First-person CTA copy ("Sign me up", "Start my trial") outperforms second
   person in tests ([Matchbox](https://matchboxdesigngroup.com/blog/small-words-big-impact-7-effective-microcopy-hacks-for-high-converting-ctas/),
   [UXPin](https://www.uxpin.com/studio/blog/microcopy-that-converts/)) —
   a refinement the classics never tested.
7. Reassurance microcopy near the button moves real money: +11.3% checkout
   conversions from "there will be no additional costs"
   ([Kissmetrics](https://kissmetrics.io/blog/cta-button-best-practices),
   Yoast case).
8. One-field email forms convert best (2.1% vs 1.4% at 5+ fields); ≤3 fields
   ~25% better ([OptiMonk](https://www.optimonk.com/email-capture-best-practices),
   [Omnisend](https://www.omnisend.com/blog/email-capture/)).
9. Discount-bearing capture popups: 7.45% vs 4.6% without
   ([OptiMonk](https://www.optimonk.com/email-capture-best-practices)).
   **Contradiction:** the classic UX studies rank popups a top distraction
   and abandonment cause. Resolution: popups earn their keep only with a real
   incentive, tested timing (exit-intent or scroll-depth), never mid-checkout.
10. **Contradiction — the fold:** 10 Keys (2012) says "avoid making the user
    scroll for anything." Current practice: long pages routinely win for
    unaware/problem-aware traffic; what must be above the fold is the
    headline, value proposition, and first CTA — then repeat the CTA down the
    page ([Leadpages](https://leadpages.com/landing-pages-guide/copywriting-for-landing-pages),
    [Unbounce](https://unbounce.com/conversion-rate-optimization/the-state-of-saas-landing-pages/)).
    This is really Schwartz's rule wearing a screen: page length should match
    awareness distance, not a fold.
11. **Contradiction — urgency:** modern advice pushes limited-time discounts
    for urgency ([Crazy Egg](https://www.crazyegg.com/blog/landing-page-copy/)).
    Schwartz warns price means nothing to an unaware market and Ogilvy warns
    constant markdowns teach people to only buy on sale. Use urgency only at
    the most-aware stage, and only when the deadline is true.

## Worked micro-example: free media-kit maker for creators

Product: a free tool that builds a shareable media kit (stats, audience,
rates) for creators pitching brands. Two traffic temperatures, two heroes —
same product, different awareness stage.

**Most-aware variant** (traffic from "media kit maker" searches, retargeting,
a comparison post — they know the tool category and roughly know you):

> **H1:** Your media kit, ready in 3 minutes — free
> **Sub:** Connect your accounts once. Get a live page with your real
> follower counts, engagement rate, and rates — always up to date, ready to
> send to any brand.
> **CTA button:** Create my free media kit
> **Under button:** No credit card. No design skills. Yours to share in 3 minutes.

Why: stage-1/2 traffic gets product + offer + a removed limitation (speed,
price, "always up to date" mechanism). Brand-level claim war ("beautiful
media kits") is stage 2-3 — the auto-updating mechanism is the stage-3 move.

**Problem-aware variant** (traffic from a post about getting ignored by
brands — they feel the pain, don't know a tool exists):

> **H1:** Brands aren't ignoring you. They're ignoring your pitch.
> **Sub:** Marketers skim 100 creator emails a day and reply to the ones with
> proof: audience, engagement, past work — on one page. Here's how to be that
> one page.
> **CTA button:** Show me how it works
> **Under button:** Free · takes 3 minutes · your stats update themselves

Why: leads with the named problem and an identification hook, not the
product; the CTA sells the next step (information), not the tool; the
Schwartz move is "projecting the result of the problem" plus a mechanism tease.

## Copy review checklist

Before shipping any conversion copy:

- [ ] One desire chosen — and it's the strongest available (urgency ×
      recurrence × scope), not just the first one written
- [ ] Awareness stage diagnosed for THIS traffic source; headline starts
      where the reader is (product / claim / desire / problem / identity)
- [ ] Sophistication checked against live competitors; if they all shout the
      claim, you lead with mechanism or identity
- [ ] Headline promises a benefit or names the problem — specific, no puns,
      no blind cleverness; a number or named prospect where possible
- [ ] Message match: page headline echoes the ad/link that brought them
- [ ] Every sentence pulls the next; first paragraph could not be deleted
- [ ] Benefits before features; each feature translated to "which means…"
- [ ] Specifics over superlatives; at least one piece of proof (testimonial,
      count, result) near the first CTA
- [ ] One CTA, verb + payoff, first person; risk-killing microcopy beside it
- [ ] Price/cost stated (or "free" made explicit); no surprise costs later
- [ ] Form asks the minimum; sensitive fields last; the form headline says
      what they get and when
- [ ] Reading level ~grade 6-8; spoken language; short sentences and paragraphs
- [ ] No exit links, no competing offers, no dead-weight sections
- [ ] Zero spelling/grammar errors; squint test passed (headline → CTA)
- [ ] A/B plan: what's being tested next, headline first
