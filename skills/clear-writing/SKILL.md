# Clear Writing

## Overview

Business writing must change what a specific reader thinks or does. Larry McEnerney's point: readers do not value what you know. They value writing that shows them a problem they care about, then helps with it. And "readers get to decide, not writers" what counts as valuable.

Weak drafts explain the topic in the order the writer learned it, and never say why it matters to this reader.

## The Value Test

Check the opening for McEnerney's three parts of a problem:

1. **Instability.** Something is wrong, inconsistent, missing, unresolved, or in conflict.
2. **Cost to the reader.** What leaving it alone costs *this reader*, or what fixing it gains them. Not your cost. "Why should the reader care about your costs?"
3. **A specific reader.** Not "stakeholders". "The CFO, who signs off on Q3 spend."

Two checks:

- **Problem words.** Does the opening contain "but", "however", "inconsistent", "risk", "missing", "cost"? McEnerney shows two versions of one abstract. The first lists facts. The second adds "Nonetheless", "However" and "anomaly", and only it creates a reason to read. No problem words usually means you wrote background.
- **World problem or reader problem?** "Churn is rising across the industry" lives in the world. "Renewals fell 8 points, and your Q4 target assumed they would hold" lives in the reader. A world problem can hook. It cannot replace the reader's problem.

Background (history, definitions, method) makes the reader feel settled. Your job is to unsettle. "There will be world enough and time for background after you've constructed a motivating problem."

## Opening Recipe

All required, in this order:

1. **What they think is stable** (skip only if they already feel the problem). "We planned Q4 assuming the vendor contract renews at the same price."
2. **The instability, with a problem word.** "But the price rises 40% on January 1."
3. **The cost, in their terms.** "That puts your support budget $60,000 over, in the quarter you report to the board."
4. **Your answer.** "I recommend we sign with the second vendor by November 15."
5. **What you need, by when.** "I need your approval by Friday."

Then evidence, main point above supporting points. End with who does what by when. Cut closers like "Please call if you have any questions." For email, the subject line is the opening: make it useful, urgent, unique and ultra-specific.

## Clutter Rules

"Clutter is the disease of American writing." (Zinsser) "If it isn't essential, cut it out." (Roman and Raphaelson)

| Rule | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| Numbers, not adjectives | Our adult program was a great success. | Our enrollment doubled to 560. |
| State the consequence | Fault tolerance to local loop failure left a lot to be desired. | If the network goes down, the company goes belly up. |
| People doing things | Evaluative procedures for the objectives were also established based on acceptable criteria. | At the end of the year we will evaluate our progress. |
| Cut hedges | And yet, on balance, affirmative action has, I think, been a qualified success. | Five hedges in 13 words. Decide, then say it. |
| Say it directly | The situation in regard to France is very serious. | The news from France is bad. |
| Short words | at this point in time / to impact | now / to affect |

If you would not say it to this reader across a desk, do not write it.

## Revision Procedure

Writing to think and writing for readers are different texts. The first draft follows the order you figured things out. The reader needs the order that wins them over.

1. **Draft fast.** Write a one-sentence claim, then draft to the end without polishing. "Your draft should be drafty." (Elfenbein)
2. **Rest,** overnight if you can.
3. **Fix the argument first.** Reverse outline: one line per paragraph saying what it does. Cut what does not move the reader toward the ask.
4. **Rewrite the opening last.** The real problem often appears only after the body. Rerun the Value Test.
5. **Bracket and cut.** Bracket every word doing no work, read without it. Zinsser says most first drafts can lose half.
6. **Read aloud.** Fix anything stiff or confusing.
7. **Test on a reader.** Have them say your aim back. Ask "What is missing?", not only "What is unclear?" If several readers stumble on one section, the section is the problem: cut or rewrite it.
8. **Spend extra drafts where the result is decided:** opening, ask, subject line. Hormozi wrote 19 drafts of *$100M Leads*, restarting after draft 12. One subtitle word moved his test from 29% to 71%.

## Common Mistakes

- **Background first.** Context and "as you know" before the problem. Move it after, or cut it.
- **Your costs, not theirs.** "My team is stretched" is yours. "Your launch slips two weeks" is theirs.
- **Gap claims.** "No one has looked at X" matters only if the gap costs the reader something.
- **Skipping the problem because "they know."** Stating it costs a sentence. Skipping it can end the reading.
- **Overselling.** One exaggeration makes readers doubt the rest. Understate.
- **Keeping material because it took work.** If it does not change what the reader does, cut it.

- **The Problem of the Problem** (McEnerney): problem parts, background versus stasis, gap claims, problem words, opening last, "what is missing?"
- **Writing That Works** (Roman and Raphaelson): action as the goal, point first, next step last, clutter examples, understatement.
