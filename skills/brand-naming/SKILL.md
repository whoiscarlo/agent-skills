# Brand Naming

## Overview

There is almost never one perfect name. Do not hunt for it. Build a strategy, generate widely, then pick the best of a shortlist that everyone understands.

Thimble, an insurance startup, shows why. The name was number three on a list of 10. It took two more rounds before the team saw it was the right one.

## The Process

1. **Write the brief before any names.** Answer: who is the audience, what is the offer, and does the name need to stand out or fit in? First Direct stood out against stuffy bank names. Stratton Oakmont was picked to sound bland and established. Decide which one or two things the name must say. A name can only say a few things. The logo, copy and products carry the rest.

2. **Map the competitor names.** No name means anything on its own. It only means something next to the competition. List real names in the market and group them by pattern (for example founder names, the word "Capital", premium words, acronyms). Note trends to follow or avoid. Search the web if you can. If not, say the map is from memory.

3. **Turn the brief into territories.** Use four questions: what is the core offer, what does the brand stand for, how will it make people feel, why does it matter? Treat territories as "signposts, not fence posts".

4. **Generate widely, across name types.** Aim for dozens before judging any. Cover every type:
   - Descriptive (Whole Foods)
   - Made up (Kodak, 7-Up)
   - People's names (Marcus)
   - Blends (Netflix, Pinterest)
   - Parts of words (Pepsi, from dyspepsia)
   - Purpose (Innocent, Impossible Burger)
   - Associations (Nike, Uber)
   - Acronyms
   - Just fun (Moonpig)

   Let word associations drift far. Security leads to keys, then quay, then boats, then Marlin. Walls lead to wood, forest, fairy tales and Beanstalk. When stuck, use odd prompts: only one starting letter, only animal names, the worst possible name, or how you would name it if it were a car or a bank. Write first, edit later.

5. **Cut to a shortlist of 10 to 15.** Judge each name against the brief and the competitor map, not against gut feel. "It reminds me of a dog I once had" is not a reason. Be careful with made-up words for small brands. They need a big budget to become familiar, and people struggle to remember or say them.

6. **Run rounds.** Every rejected name is rejected for a reason. Write it down. Each "no" tells you more about the name you need.

7. **Run the checks.** Google the name plus the industry. Use a trademark checker (gov.uk for the UK, TMview for global). Then an IP lawyer. Check other languages with wordsafety.com (19 languages); remember the Ford Pinto in Brazil. Check domains (namecheck.com). A .com is not essential.

## Required Output for "Give Me Your Best Name"

Never answer with a single name and a pun list. Even in one reply, use this shape:

1. **Strategy line.** One or two sentences: audience, offer, stand out or fit in, the one or two things the name must say.
2. **Competitor landscape.** Real names in the space, grouped by pattern, plus the gap you are aiming at. Flag anything not verified.
3. **Shortlist grouped by type.** Several types, 10 to 15 names total. For each, one line tying it to the strategy and saying how it sits against competitors.
4. **Recommendation, with what would change it.** Name your lead pick and the runner-up. Say what new fact would flip the choice (for example, "if the brand must sound clinical, pick X instead").
5. **Checks still needed.** Trademark, translation, domain, and any competitor clash you could not confirm. Do not claim a name is clear when you have not checked.

Then ask which names they reject and why. That starts the next round.

## Presenting Names So People Judge Fairly

- Never show a name cold, alone in an email or on a slide.
- Walk through the strategy first, so the pick feels logical.
- Show each name in context: a sample sentence, a mock home page, a business card, or answering the phone with it.
- Add a short rationale and domain notes. Do not add so much design that people react to colours instead of the name.
- Keep it focused. A list of hundreds feels unconsidered.
- Give people time to sleep on it. The third favourite often wins weeks later.
- Not everyone will love it. Doubts usually fade within a year.
- Be wary of focus groups. They tend to leave only the most boring names. Memorability can be tested.

## Renaming

Renaming is harder. More people are involved and some love the old name. Without a forced reason, such as a trademark dispute, people ask "do we have to?". Show the new name is clearly better.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Fix |
|---|---|
| Naming a winner in the first line | Brief and competitor map first |
| Puns the category already uses (pet brands and "purr") | Check the competitor map for clichés before shortlisting |
| Only one or two name types | Generate across every type in step 4 |
| Judging by your own taste | Score each name against the brief |
| One round and done | Expect rounds; log each rejection reason |
