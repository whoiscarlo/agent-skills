# Brand Voice

A brand voice is what you say and how you say it, to a specific reader, the same way everywhere. It is not "fairy dust" sprinkled over finished copy. The one word to keep in mind is **specific**. A voice that lives as adjectives in a PDF falls apart the first time a support agent or a lawyer writes in it.

Siblings: `conversion-copy` (headlines, page structure), `sales-psychology`, `brand-naming`, `clear-writing`.

## The core tests (run on every sentence)

- [ ] **Can I visualize it?** Keep asking "what do I actually mean?" until you reach a thing you can see. "Regain fitness" becomes "Couch to 5K".
- [ ] **Can I falsify it?** Could a reader check it true or false? "Good looking and intelligent" becomes "Six foot two, reads on the tube." Imagine you cannot talk, only point.
- [ ] **Could nobody else say it?** If a competitor could sign it, rewrite. Volvo: "Your car has five numbers on the speedometer. Volvo has six."
- [ ] **One idea?** Does every line serve one big idea, or is it "tossed salad copy"?
- [ ] **Their words?** Is the wording taken from what buyers actually say, or from the company's own description of itself?
- [ ] **Same brand?** Read it aloud next to an ad and a support email. Does it sound like one company?

## Building a voice guideline

Start by finding the voice, not describing it.

1. **Gather every sample**, from ads to T&Cs. Read them aloud. Do they sound like one brand?
2. **Write two or three very different sample voices in real copy**, such as an "about us" intro. Ask which exact phrases people like, not which qualities. Merge those into one voice.
3. **No strategy yet?** Write a bad news email (an order that will arrive late). Cold and formal, or warm? The answer tells you who the brand is.

Then write the guideline like a recipe book. These parts are REQUIRED:

1. **A one-line foundation.** Schuh's is "weirdly relatable".
2. **One to three character words, each with do and don't rewrites.** A word alone is useless. Nike, HSBC and Oatly are all "human" in very different ways. Under each word, show a real line that fits and one that does not, with a note on why.
3. **Practical tools.** Sentence length. How direct. Humor and emojis, yes or no, and where. A swap list of normal words for formal ones (Monzo does this). Banned words (UpCircle never says waste or trash; it says "re-loved ingredients with more to give"). Plain tips like "write how you chat". Skip writer jargon like "hyphenate modifiers"; non-writers fall back on old habits.
4. **Before and after examples for every touchpoint, including the boring ones**, each with a note on what changed and why. Writers skip straight to this section, so give many. Cover at least: product page, ad, packaging, order confirmation, delay email, complaint reply, newsletter signup, terms and privacy notice, job ad. Say where tone steps back: playful on Instagram and product pages, plain at checkout and in customer service.
5. **A short brief for outsiders.** Agencies left alone drift into stuffy copy. Show them what good sounds like.
6. **A training and upkeep plan.** Guidelines in a PDF die. Include:
   - Workshops for 10 to 12 key writers from marketing and customer service, who become advocates. Shorter sessions for everyone else, built on their own channel.
   - A safe room where people try the voice and get it wrong.
   - The voice in onboarding for new joiners.
   - Guardians from brand, HR, service and product meeting every month or two. Not just the head of marketing.
   - Regular review, because voices drift by department.

## One big idea

Pick one idea. Everything else exists to support it. Michael Masterson found 91 of Agora's 100 top offers were built on a single idea. A big idea is easy to understand, easy to believe, and interesting or unique. If you have six points, make them six proofs of one benefit. Spend the effort choosing the idea; then the copy is simple to write.

## Use the buyer's words

Copy is your best sales pitch, written down. Before writing, read where buyers talk: Reddit, Facebook groups, forums, and competitors' one, three and five star reviews. Copy their exact phrases about pains and hopes into one document, refreshed every six months. Assemble copy from it instead of a blank page. Keep it about 80% about the prospect. "The pain is the pitch": describe the pain in concrete moments, like standing at the side of every photo. If you describe it well enough, you barely need a promise.

For a company or startup story, say what it means for the reader, not what it is. "A network of 100,000 solar powered scooters" becomes "We're here to make using a car in the city obsolete." Lead with the problem, not the technology. Nuggets pitched a bank without once saying "blockchain".

## Luxury and premium

Never write "premium", "luxury", "exceptional quality" or "decades of experience". Readers skip them like wallpaper. Show a person, place or scene instead.

- "Only the best Swiss craftspeople make our watches. They have decades of experience." becomes "This is Matteo. He's been making watches for 40 years. Around here we call him the newbie."
- "Contains lavender and sandalwood" becomes "Lavender and sandalwood rub shoulders in the fragrant Turkish baths of Mayfair."

Luxury is not one voice: butler, storyteller, poet, show-off, minimalist and joker can all work. Pick the true one. Hold back, so the rare playful moment stands out. Do not fake heritage; a young brand can play up being new. Carry the voice into small corners, like Penhaligon's privacy notice ending "Scouts honour."

## Worked example (illustrative, dog food brand Peek)

Bracketed parts must be replaced with real, checkable facts or real customer phrases. Never invent them.

**Product line. Before:** "Peek offers premium, high-quality, nutritious food crafted with love for your furry friend."
Fails every test: nothing to see, nothing to check, any brand could print it, five claims at once.

**After:** "The bowl comes back licked clean. [N] of [N] picky eaters finished their first Peek bowl in our trial."
One idea (fussy dogs eat it), in owners' own review words, visual, checkable, and only Peek's. Other claims go lower down as proof of it.

**Support reply. Before:** "Thank you for your patience. We apologize for any inconvenience. That's not the experience we want you to have."
**After:** "Your order should have reached you Tuesday. It arrived Friday, so [dog's name] may have been short on food. I'm sorry. I've [concrete fix, such as refunding delivery] and your next bag ships today. If anything else goes wrong, reply here and I'll sort it myself. [First name], Peek"
This follows the pattern "I'm sorry you've had this problem. Here's how we can fix it." Name what happened, own it, say exactly what you are doing.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Fix |
|---|---|
| Voice defined by adjectives ("warm, plain, specific") | Each character word gets do and don't rewrites, plus examples for every touchpoint |
| Guideline handed over with no plan to train or review | Workshops, safe practice, guardians, onboarding, regular review |
| Support replies in stock corporate lines ("thank you for your patience", "we apologize for any inconvenience", "Sincerely") | Name the problem, say sorry plainly, state the fix. Nike's own delay email broke its voice this way |
| Rewrite stacks several claims ("real chicken, vet-formulated, gently cooked, and...") | Lead with one big idea; the rest becomes proof underneath |
| Copy written from the company's view | Mine reviews and forums; use the buyer's exact words |
| Polishing ads, not T&Cs or order emails | Check the boring, most-read messages first. Lloyds had warm ads and cold T&Cs |
| Copying a rival's tone | Copying Oatly made oat milk brands sound alike. Bulb won by being simple and clear |
| One clever draft | Rewrite many times (Harry Dry: about 25 rewrites of one ad; Hormozi: 19 drafts). Show three versions for feedback |
